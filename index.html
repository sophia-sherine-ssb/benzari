Matlab  ·  Fusion 360  ·  Thermodynamics  ·  2025

Phase Change Material
Heat Simulation + CAD

MATLABFusion 360Thermal SimulationThermodynamicsEnergy SystemsCAD

The Problem

Los Angeles runs on a grid not designed for the heat it now faces. During summer heat waves, electrical transformers experience extreme thermal stress — as load increases and ambient temperatures rise, they overheat, derate, and in the worst cases, fail entirely.

During the 2006–2007 LA heat storms, more than 800 transformers failed, cutting power to tens of thousands of residents. The problem is compounding: heat waves are intensifying, energy demand is climbing, and the infrastructure is aging.

"More than 64,000 customers experienced power outages during a heat wave, as utilities worked on overheating equipment." — LAIST

The Approach

The project proposes a passive PCM-based cooling device — a unit filled with paraffin wax that attaches directly to a pad-mounted transformer. When the wax melts, it absorbs a large quantity of heat without itself rising significantly in temperature. Overnight, as ambient temperatures drop, the wax solidifies again, releasing stored heat and resetting for the next day.

No pumps. No electricity. No maintenance. Just thermodynamics.

To validate the design, I built a thermal simulation in MATLAB modeling transformer heat behavior under realistic LA load conditions, and a 3D CAD model in Fusion 360 showing how the PCM unit integrates with the transformer housing.

CAD Design — Fusion 360

Rendered model of the PCM cooling unit integrated with a pad-mounted transformer. The grey panel is the paraffin wax housing. Designed in Fusion 360.

The CAD model places the PCM enclosure in direct thermal contact with the transformer's outer casing. The design accounts for the ~10% volume expansion during melting, uses standard mounting hardware for retrofit compatibility, and includes ventilation slots for overnight heat dissipation.

Simulation Parameters

The MATLAB simulation models heat flow across two coupled systems over an 8-hour cycle representative of a Los Angeles summer afternoon.

Partial melting is often desirable. If the PCM never fully melts, it retains buffering capacity throughout the peak period — continuing to absorb heat rather than reaching thermal saturation.

Simulation Results

Temperature over time — with PCM (red) vs without (blue dashed). Peak temperature reduced by ~25°C.

Heat flow paths: transformer to PCM to air. Sharp peak at hour 5 reflects the evening temperature drop.

During hours 2–5, transformer heat output rises sharply to 10,000 W. Without PCM, surface temperature climbs past 80°C. With the PCM unit attached, peak temperature is held below 58°C. From hour 5 onward, ambient temperature drops from 32°C to 24°C — the PCM releases its stored heat and resets overnight.

The PCM system reduced simulated peak transformer temperature by approximately 25°C under LA heatwave conditions — demonstrating that passive phase-change cooling can meaningfully extend safe operating range without adding energy consumption.

MATLAB Simulation Code

The simulation uses an enthalpy-based lumped-parameter model with three local functions: simulatePCM, simulateNoPCM, and enthalpyToState.

heatsimadv.m  —  MATLABview only
%% pcm_transformer_demo.m
% Lumped-parameter simulation of transformer + PCM cooling
% Compares good PCM design vs no-PCM baseline

clear; clc; close all;

dt   = 1;  tEnd = 8*3600;  t = 0:dt:tEnd;
th   = t/3600;
qGen = 3500 + 6500 * (t >= 2*3600 & t <= 5*3600);
Tamb = 32 * ones(size(t));  Tamb(t >= 5*3600) = 24;

params.Ctr=500000; params.Tinit=32; params.mPCM=180;
params.cpPCM=2200; params.L=180000; params.Tm=55; params.Tref=0;
good.Rtp=0.0005; good.Rpa=0.002;
noPCM.Rta=0.005;

outGood = simulatePCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, params, good);
outNo   = simulateNoPCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, params, noPCM);

figure('Color','w'); hold on;
plot(th, outNo.Ttr,    '--', 'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.0 0.45 0.74]);
plot(th, outGood.Ttr,  '-',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.85 0.33 0.10]);
plot(th, outGood.Tpcm, '-',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.93 0.69 0.13]);
plot(th, Tamb,         ':',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.49 0.18 0.56]);
grid on; legend('No PCM','With PCM','PCM temp','Ambient','Location','northwest');
title('Transformer Cooling With PCM');

function out = simulatePCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, p, d)
    n=numel(t); Ttr=zeros(1,n); Tpcm=zeros(1,n);
    x=zeros(1,n); qTP=zeros(1,n); qPA=zeros(1,n);
    Ttr(1)=p.Tinit;
    Hpcm=p.mPCM*p.cpPCM*(p.Tinit-p.Tref);
    [Tpcm(1),x(1)]=enthalpyToState(Hpcm,p);
    for k=2:n
        qTP(k-1)=(Ttr(k-1)-Tpcm(k-1))/d.Rtp;
        qPA(k-1)=(Tpcm(k-1)-Tamb(k-1))/d.Rpa;
        Ttr(k)=Ttr(k-1)+(qGen(k-1)-qTP(k-1))*dt/p.Ctr;
        Hpcm=Hpcm+(qTP(k-1)-qPA(k-1))*dt;
        [Tpcm(k),x(k)]=enthalpyToState(Hpcm,p);
    end
    qTP(end)=(Ttr(end)-Tpcm(end))/d.Rtp;
    qPA(end)=(Tpcm(end)-Tamb(end))/d.Rpa;
    out.Ttr=Ttr; out.Tpcm=Tpcm; out.meltFrac=x; out.qTP=qTP; out.qPA=qPA;
end

function out = simulateNoPCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, p, n0)
    n=numel(t); Ttr=zeros(1,n); Ttr(1)=p.Tinit;
    for k=2:n
        qToAir=(Ttr(k-1)-Tamb(k-1))/n0.Rta;
        Ttr(k)=Ttr(k-1)+(qGen(k-1)-qToAir)*dt/p.Ctr;
    end
    out.Ttr=Ttr;
end

function [T,x] = enthalpyToState(H, p)
    HsolidEnd=p.mPCM*p.cpPCM*(p.Tm-p.Tref);
    HliqEnd=HsolidEnd+p.mPCM*p.L;
    if H < HsolidEnd
        T=p.Tref+H/(p.mPCM*p.cpPCM); x=0;
    elseif H <= HliqEnd
        T=p.Tm; x=(H-HsolidEnd)/(p.mPCM*p.L);
    else
        T=p.Tm+(H-HliqEnd)/(p.mPCM*p.cpPCM); x=1;
    end
    x=max(0,min(1,x));
end

Enthalpy-based lumped-parameter model with three local functions: simulatePCM, simulateNoPCM, and enthalpyToState.

Reflections

The most striking takeaway was how much cooling capacity can be unlocked from a material's phase transition alone. The latent heat of paraffin wax — 180,000 J/kg — is roughly fifty times larger than its sensible heat capacity over a 10°C range, meaning a well-chosen PCM can absorb enormous amounts of thermal energy while barely changing temperature.

Simulation also clarified how important partial melting is as a design goal. A system that fully melts its PCM early has no remaining buffer for the rest of the peak period. The right combination of mass, melting point, and thermal resistance keeps the PCM actively absorbing heat for the entire high-load window.

This project was developed alongside Lehar Goenka, exploring how passive thermal systems could address infrastructure resilience in heat-stressed cities.

Cochlear Implant
Vocoder Simulation

MATLABSignal ProcessingBiomedicalAuditory PerceptionFilter BanksHilbert Transform

Overview

This project explores how cochlear implants transform sound — and what that transformation means for human perception. Cochlear implants don't restore normal hearing. Instead, they deliver a highly simplified version of sound by breaking it into a small number of frequency channels and transmitting only the most essential information.

While this is often enough for speech understanding, it comes at the cost of spectral detail — making things like pitch and music much harder to perceive. To better understand this process, I built a cochlear implant vocoder in MATLAB, simulating how audio is encoded and reconstructed under these constraints.

This work was completed under the supervision of Raymond Goldsworthy in the Cochlear Implant Lab at USC, informed by research on auditory perception and multisensory integration.

The Problem

Cochlear implants face a fundamental limitation: they must compress a rich, continuous audio signal into a small number of discrete channels. In a normal ear, thousands of hair cells respond to fine frequency differences. A cochlear implant typically uses only 12–22 electrodes, dramatically reducing spectral resolution.

The result is uneven — speech can remain intelligible, but pitch perception becomes unreliable and music sounds distorted or unrecognizable.

Temporal envelopes are enough for speech. They are not enough for rich auditory experience.

Signal Processing Pipeline

Both vocoders follow the same four-stage architecture, mirroring how cochlear implants process sound in real devices:

StageOperationPurpose
1 — Filter BankBandpass filter per channelDivide audio into frequency bands simulating electrodes
2 — Envelope ExtractionHilbert transformCapture slow amplitude fluctuations carrying speech
3 — Low-pass SmoothingLP filter at 400 HzRemove fine structure, keep only temporal envelope
4 — Carrier Modulationenvelope × sine (or noise)Re-synthesize output with pitch information removed

The multi-channel version implements Greenwood frequency spacing — a mathematically derived mapping that mirrors the actual tonotopic organisation of the human cochlea, making the simulation biologically accurate.

MATLAB Code — Multi-Channel Vocoder

The multi-channel implementation is configurable — number of channels, frequency spacing (including Greenwood), envelope extraction technique, and carrier type can all be adjusted.

multichannelvocoder.m  —  MATLABview only
clc; clear; close all;
[file,path]=uigetfile('*.wav'); [x,fs]=audioread(fullfile(path,file));
x=mean(x,2); t=(0:length(x)-1)'/fs;

numChannels=8; f_low=100; f_high=min(8000,fs/2-100);
spacing="greenwood"; useHilbert=true; carrierType="sine"; fc_env=400;

A=165.4; a=2.1; k=0.88;
x_low=(1/a)*log10(f_low/A+k); x_high=(1/a)*log10(f_high/A+k);
x_pos=linspace(x_low,x_high,numChannels+1);
edges=A*(10.^(a*x_pos)-k);   % Greenwood cochlear frequency map

env_lp_all=zeros(length(x),numChannels);
y_bands=zeros(length(x),numChannels);

for k=1:numChannels
    band=[edges(k) edges(k+1)];
    x_bp=bandpass(x,band,fs);
    if useHilbert; env=abs(hilbert(x_bp)); else; env=abs(x_bp); end
    env_lp=lowpass(env,fc_env,fs);
    env_lp_all(:,k)=env_lp;
    fc_k=sqrt(edges(k)*edges(k+1));
    carrier=sin(2*pi*fc_k*t);
    y_bands(:,k)=env_lp.*carrier;
end

y_out=sum(y_bands,2); y_out=y_out./(max(abs(y_out))+eps);
audiowrite('multichannel_vocoded.wav',y_out,fs);

figure; subplot(2,1,1); spectrogram(x,512,384,1024,fs,'yaxis'); title('Original');
         subplot(2,1,2); spectrogram(y_out,512,384,1024,fs,'yaxis'); title('Vocoded');

Multi-channel vocoder with Greenwood spacing, Hilbert envelope extraction, and sine carriers.

Reflections

This project was less about building the code and more about understanding the system behind it. The key realisation was how asymmetric the information requirements for hearing actually are — temporal envelopes, which carry very little data, are sufficient for speech intelligibility. But the fine spectral structure that envelopes discard is exactly what makes music and pitch recognisable.

Working in the Cochlear Implant Lab also introduced me to the idea of multisensory integration — the hypothesis that combining auditory and tactile signals could improve pitch perception for cochlear implant users. This connects the simulation work directly to ongoing experimental research.

Electric Guitar —
Wood Component

WoodworkingBandsawTable RouterHand ToolsFinishingUSC EE 202L

Overview

This is the wood component of a larger dual-part project for EE 202L — Linear Circuits at USC. The course asked us to design and build a fully functional electric guitar from scratch, embedding a custom-designed PCB with a 196 Hz notch filter inside the guitar body.

The project had two deeply intertwined halves: the woodwork (this page) and the electronics — filter design, PCB layout in KiCad, soldering, and assembly. This page focuses on the fabrication of the guitar body and neck, done independently in the USC Maker Space.

Two weeks of work. 23 pieces of recycled wood. 20 hours of fabrication. $45 in materials. One fully playable purple electric guitar.

System Architecture

The guitar is a complete electro-acoustic system. The pickup captures string vibration, feeds it through the notch filter PCB housed in the blue box, and outputs filtered audio through a jack to an external speaker.

Signal chain from string to speaker. The blue box — containing the notch filter PCB, battery, LED, and switch — lives inside the routed cavity of the guitar body.

Wood Fabrication — The Body

No wood was purchased for this project. The guitar body was built entirely from 23 pieces of recycled wood — offcuts left over from other students' guitars in the Maker Space. Each piece had to be cut to an even height and width, jointed on both faces to be perfectly flat and straight, then glued together and left overnight to cure.

Once the blank was ready, a bandsaw was used to cut the overall body shape. The grain direction was chosen deliberately — with the grain, not against it — to maximise durability. A table router gave the edges their rounded profile. The design was modeled after Prince's iconic guitar shape, chosen from the start.

Early stage — body and neck pre-sanding, post-bandsaw. The blue rectangles mark the cavity and box positions.

Post-table router — the rounded edges are visible. The body cavity for the blue box has been roughed out.

Wood Fabrication — The Neck

The neck was provided as raw stock in class. The headstock was bent and shaped to match the guitar's design — which ruled out piano pins entirely, since they are not suited for a bent headstock. Guitar tuning pegs were used instead. A channel was routed along the side of the neck to run the cable from the pickup into the blue box inside the body.

Fret slots were carved by hand using a saw and chisel. The fretboard came pre-slotted in walnut. Fitting the neck into the body pocket required multiple rounds of test-fitting, carving, and adjusting.

Neck blank alongside the walnut fretboard — checking fit and alignment before routing the cable channel.

Neck with fret slots carved and gold tuning pegs installed. Chisel and file visible — fret work was entirely by hand.

Details, Cracks & Repairs

Sanding revealed cracks in the wood that weren't visible before. Each crack was sealed with a mixture of wood glue and fine sawdust — pressed in, left to cure, then sanded flush. The headstock area was particularly affected and required careful taping and clamping during repairs.

The body was inspected and sanded multiple times by different people in the Maker Space. Every pass revealed something new. This part of the process alone took several hours.

Headstock crack sealed with glue and wood dust, held under blue painter's tape while curing.

Neck after final sanding — the profile is smooth and the wood grain is clean and even.

Painting — Purple & Gold

The guitar was modeled after Prince — so purple and gold was the only choice. Krylon spray paint was applied in multiple coats, with the guitar left overnight between sessions to let the wood fully absorb the paint. The paint ran out halfway through and had to be replaced mid-session.

Every detail was painted gold: frets, nut, screws, the top face of the blue box, and the back of the neck. An actual gold bridge replaced the standard string screws. Holes were drilled into the top of the blue box for the jack, LED, and switch. The finished guitar weighs approximately 13 pounds.

Reflections

This project was unexpected in the best way. It started as a circuits course and turned into a full woodworking and design challenge. Building something physical that works — that you can plug into a speaker and play — is a different kind of satisfaction than a simulation result on a screen.

Using recycled wood was a constraint that became a source of pride. The grain matching, the jointing, the hours of sanding — none of it was required, but all of it mattered. The guitar received a lot of compliments. But the more meaningful thing was learning that engineering and craft are not separate disciplines.

The electrical half of this project — the filter design, PCB layout in KiCad, soldering, and final assembly — will be documented separately as the Electric Guitar (PCB with 196 Hz Notch Filter) project.
