The Problem

Los Angeles runs on a grid not designed for the heat it now faces. During summer heat waves, electrical transformers experience extreme thermal stress — as load increases and ambient temperatures rise, they overheat, derate, and in the worst cases, fail entirely.

During the 2006–2007 LA heat storms, more than 800 transformers failed, cutting power to tens of thousands of residents. The problem is compounding: heat waves are intensifying, energy demand is climbing, and the infrastructure is aging.

"More than 64,000 customers experienced power outages during a heat wave, as utilities worked on overheating equipment." — LAIST

The Approach

The project proposes a passive PCM-based cooling device — a unit filled with paraffin wax that attaches directly to a pad-mounted transformer. When the wax melts, it absorbs a large quantity of heat without itself rising significantly in temperature. Overnight, as ambient temperatures drop, the wax solidifies again, releasing stored heat and resetting for the next day.

No pumps. No electricity. No maintenance. Just thermodynamics.

To validate the design, I built a thermal simulation in MATLAB modeling transformer heat behavior under realistic LA load conditions, and a 3D CAD model in Fusion 360 showing how the PCM unit integrates with the transformer housing.

CAD Design — Fusion 360

Rendered model of the PCM cooling unit integrated with a pad-mounted transformer. The grey panel is the paraffin wax housing. Designed in Fusion 360.

The CAD model places the PCM enclosure in direct thermal contact with the transformer's outer casing. The design accounts for the ~10% volume expansion during melting, uses standard mounting hardware for retrofit compatibility, and includes ventilation slots for overnight heat dissipation.

Simulation Results

Temperature over time — with PCM (red) vs without (blue dashed). Peak temperature reduced by ~25°C.

Heat flow paths: transformer → PCM → air. Sharp peak at hour 5 reflects the evening temperature drop.

During hours 2–5, transformer heat output rises sharply to 10,000 W. Without PCM, surface temperature climbs past 80°C. With the PCM unit attached, peak temperature is held below 58°C. From hour 5 onward, ambient temperature drops from 32°C to 24°C — the PCM releases its stored heat and resets overnight.

The PCM system reduced simulated peak transformer temperature by approximately 25°C under LA heatwave conditions — demonstrating that passive phase-change cooling can meaningfully extend safe operating range without adding energy consumption.

MATLAB Simulation Code

The simulation uses an enthalpy-based lumped-parameter model — more physically accurate than a simple forward Euler approach. Three local functions handle the PCM state, the baseline, and the enthalpy-to-temperature conversion.

heatsimadv.m  —  MATLAB view only 
%% pcm_transformer_demo.m
% Lumped-parameter simulation of transformer + PCM cooling
% Compares good PCM design vs no-PCM baseline

clear; clc; close all;

%% Time
dt   = 1;           % time step [s]
tEnd = 8*3600;     % 8 hours [s]
t    = 0:dt:tEnd;
n    = numel(t);
th   = t/3600;      % time in hours for plotting

%% Heat input from transformer losses [W]
% Step-like peak load during hours 2 to 5
qGen = 3500 + 6500 * (t >= 2*3600 & t <= 5*3600);

%% Ambient temperature [degC]
% Hot afternoon first, then cooler evening
Tamb = 32 * ones(size(t));
Tamb(t >= 5*3600) = 24;

%% Common transformer properties
params.Ctr   = 500000;   % thermal capacitance [J/K]
params.Tinit = 32;       % initial temperature [degC]

%% PCM properties
params.mPCM  = 180;      % PCM mass [kg]
params.cpPCM = 2200;     % sensible heat capacity [J/(kg*K)]
params.L     = 180000;   % latent heat [J/kg]
params.Tm    = 55;       % melting temperature [degC]
params.Tref  = 0;        % reference temperature [degC]

%% Good PCM design parameters
good.Rtp = 0.0005;   % transformer -> PCM resistance [K/W]
good.Rpa = 0.002;    % PCM -> ambient resistance [K/W]

%% No PCM baseline
noPCM.Rta = 0.005;   % transformer -> ambient resistance [K/W]

%% Simulate
outGood = simulatePCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, params, good);
outNo   = simulateNoPCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, params, noPCM);

%% Figure 1: Temperatures
figure('Color','w');
plot(th, outNo.Ttr,    '--', 'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.0 0.45 0.74]); hold on;
plot(th, outGood.Ttr,  '-',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.85 0.33 0.10]);
plot(th, outGood.Tpcm, '-',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.93 0.69 0.13]);
plot(th, Tamb,         ':',  'LineWidth', 2, 'Color', [0.49 0.18 0.56]);
grid on;
xlabel('Time [hours]'); ylabel('Temperature [degC]');
title('Transformer Cooling With PCM');
legend('No PCM transformer', 'Transformer with PCM', ...
       'PCM temperature', 'Ambient air', 'Location', 'northwest');

%% Figure 2: Heat flows and melt fraction
figure('Color','w');
subplot(2,1,1);
plot(th, qGen/1000,        'k', 'LineWidth', 2); hold on;
plot(th, outGood.qTP/1000, '-', 'LineWidth', 2);
plot(th, outGood.qPA/1000, '-', 'LineWidth', 2);
grid on;
xlabel('Time [hours]'); ylabel('Heat flow [kW]');
title('Heat Flow Paths — PCM Case');
legend('Transformer heat generation', 'Transformer -> PCM', ...
       'PCM -> Air', 'Location', 'northwest');

subplot(2,1,2);
plot(th, outGood.meltFrac, 'LineWidth', 2);
grid on;
xlabel('Time [hours]'); ylabel('Melt fraction');
ylim([0 1.05]);
title('PCM Melt Fraction (0 = solid, 1 = fully melted)');

%% ---- Local functions ----
function out = simulatePCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, p, d)
    n = numel(t);
    Ttr  = zeros(1,n); Tpcm = zeros(1,n);
    x    = zeros(1,n); qTP  = zeros(1,n); qPA  = zeros(1,n);
    Ttr(1) = p.Tinit;
    Hpcm = p.mPCM * p.cpPCM * (p.Tinit - p.Tref);
    [Tpcm(1), x(1)] = enthalpyToState(Hpcm, p);
    for k = 2:n
        qTP(k-1) = (Ttr(k-1) - Tpcm(k-1)) / d.Rtp;
        qPA(k-1) = (Tpcm(k-1) - Tamb(k-1)) / d.Rpa;
        dTtr = (qGen(k-1) - qTP(k-1)) * dt / p.Ctr;
        Ttr(k) = Ttr(k-1) + dTtr;
        Hpcm = Hpcm + (qTP(k-1) - qPA(k-1)) * dt;
        [Tpcm(k), x(k)] = enthalpyToState(Hpcm, p);
    end
    qTP(end) = (Ttr(end) - Tpcm(end)) / d.Rtp;
    qPA(end) = (Tpcm(end) - Tamb(end)) / d.Rpa;
    out.Ttr = Ttr; out.Tpcm = Tpcm;
    out.meltFrac = x; out.qTP = qTP; out.qPA = qPA;
end

function out = simulateNoPCM(t, dt, qGen, Tamb, p, n0)
    n = numel(t); Ttr = zeros(1,n); Ttr(1) = p.Tinit;
    for k = 2:n
        qToAir = (Ttr(k-1) - Tamb(k-1)) / n0.Rta;
        Ttr(k) = Ttr(k-1) + (qGen(k-1) - qToAir) * dt / p.Ctr;
    end
    out.Ttr = Ttr;
end

function [T, x] = enthalpyToState(H, p)
    % Convert PCM enthalpy H into temperature T and melt fraction x
    HsolidEnd = p.mPCM * p.cpPCM * (p.Tm - p.Tref);
    HliqEnd   = HsolidEnd + p.mPCM * p.L;
    if H < HsolidEnd
        T = p.Tref + H / (p.mPCM * p.cpPCM); x = 0;
    elseif H <= HliqEnd
        T = p.Tm; x = (H - HsolidEnd) / (p.mPCM * p.L);
    else
        T = p.Tm + (H - HliqEnd) / (p.mPCM * p.cpPCM); x = 1;
    end
    x = max(0, min(1, x));
end

Enthalpy-based lumped-parameter model with three local functions: simulatePCM, simulateNoPCM, and enthalpyToState.

%% PCM Transformer Cooling Simulation
% Sophia Benzari — Biomedical Engineering, USC
% Models heat flow between transformer, PCM unit, and ambient air
% over an 8-hour Los Angeles summer afternoon load cycle.

clear; clc; close all;

%% ── Parameters ──────────────────────────────────────────
dt    = 1;           % time step [s]
t_end = 8 * 3600;  % 8 hours [s]
t     = 0:dt:t_end;

% Transformer
C_T  = 500000;  % thermal capacitance [J/K]
T_T0 = 32;      % initial temperature [°C]
R_TP = 0.002;   % thermal resistance, transformer → PCM [K/W]
R_TA = 0.01;    % thermal resistance, transformer → air [K/W]

% PCM — paraffin wax
m_PCM  = 180;     % mass [kg]
T_melt = 55;      % melting point [°C]
L_PCM  = 180000;  % latent heat [J/kg]
Cp_PCM = 2200;    % sensible heat capacity [J/(kg·K)]
Q_lat  = m_PCM * L_PCM;  % total latent energy = 14.4 MJ
R_PA   = 0.005;   % thermal resistance, PCM → air [K/W]

% Ambient: drops 32°C → 24°C at hour 5
T_amb = 32 * ones(1, length(t));
T_amb(t >= 5*3600) = 24;

% Heat generation: idle 3500 W, peak 10000 W hrs 2–5
Q_gen = 3500 * ones(1, length(t));
Q_gen(t >= 2*3600 & t < 5*3600) = 10000;

%% ── Initialise ───────────────────────────────────────────
T_T   = zeros(1, length(t));
T_P   = zeros(1, length(t));
E_lat = zeros(1, length(t));
T_T(1) = T_T0;  T_P(1) = T_T0;

%% ── Forward Euler integration ────────────────────────────
for i = 1:length(t)-1
  q_TP = (T_T(i) - T_P(i)) / R_TP;
  q_TA = (T_T(i) - T_amb(i)) / R_TA;
  q_PA = (T_P(i) - T_amb(i)) / R_PA;

  dT_T = (Q_gen(i) - q_TP - q_TA) / C_T;
  T_T(i+1) = T_T(i) + dT_T * dt;

  Q_in = (q_TP - q_PA) * dt;
  if T_P(i) >= T_melt && E_lat(i) < Q_lat && Q_in > 0
    E_lat(i+1) = min(E_lat(i) + Q_in, Q_lat);
    T_P(i+1)   = T_melt;
  else
    E_lat(i+1) = E_lat(i);
    T_P(i+1)   = T_P(i) + Q_in / (m_PCM * Cp_PCM);
  end
end

%% ── Baseline: no PCM ────────────────────────────────────
T_noPCM = zeros(1, length(t));
T_noPCM(1) = T_T0;
for i = 1:length(t)-1
  q_out = (T_noPCM(i) - T_amb(i)) / 0.008;
  T_noPCM(i+1) = T_noPCM(i) + (Q_gen(i) - q_out) / 500000 * dt;
end

%% ── Plot ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
t_hr = t / 3600;
figure(1); hold on;
plot(t_hr, T_noPCM, 'b--', 'LineWidth', 1.8, 'DisplayName', 'No PCM');
plot(t_hr, T_T,     'r-',  'LineWidth', 1.8, 'DisplayName', 'With PCM');
plot(t_hr, T_P,     'y-',  'LineWidth', 1.4, 'DisplayName', 'PCM temp');
plot(t_hr, T_amb,   'm:',  'LineWidth', 1.2, 'DisplayName', 'Ambient');
xlabel('Time [hours]'); ylabel('Temperature [degC]');
title('Transformer Cooling With PCM'); legend; grid on;

Core simulation — forward Euler thermal model with PCM phase transition logic.

Reflections

The most striking takeaway was how much cooling capacity can be unlocked from a material's phase transition alone. The latent heat of paraffin wax — 180,000 J/kg — is roughly fifty times larger than its sensible heat capacity over a 10°C range, meaning a well-chosen PCM can absorb enormous amounts of thermal energy while barely changing temperature.

Simulation also clarified how important partial melting is as a design goal. A system that fully melts its PCM early has no remaining buffer for the rest of the peak period. The right combination of mass, melting point, and thermal resistance keeps the PCM actively absorbing heat for the entire high-load window.

This project was developed alongside Lehar Goenka, exploring how passive thermal systems could address infrastructure resilience in heat-stressed cities.
