Overview

A custom oil painting supply box, designed and built from scratch as a personal project. The brief was self-imposed: a compact, portable case that could hold a glass palette, three small glass jars for turpentine and medium, and a set of brushes — all secured, all accessible, nothing rattling around. The box had to open flat, close with a satisfying click, and look like something worth keeping.

The design went through multiple sketched iterations before moving to CAD, and the fabrication required working through joinery decisions that weren't obvious until the material was in hand. It was made with the guidance of Andrew, who runs the USC Maker Space woodshop.

A fully functional, hand-built paint box in walnut and ebonised wood, with laser-engraved lid, magnetic closure, and fitted glass palette insert.

Design Process — Sketches

The design started entirely on paper. The first sketches established the core requirements: a glass palette recessed into the base, three small glass jars seated in routed circular holders, a brush compartment along one side, and a hinged lid that opens to 180°. Magnet pairs — one set in the lid, one in the body — would hold the box closed without a latch.

The most time-consuming part of the sketch phase was working out the joinery and tolerances. The sketches show multiple iterations of the wall cross-section — testing rabbeted joints, tongue-and-groove, and dado options — to find a construction method that would allow the wood to expand and contract without splitting the joints. A rabbeted joint was selected for the frame walls, with the base panel floating inside to accommodate seasonal movement.

CAD — Fusion 360

The sketched design was modelled in Fusion 360 before any wood was cut. The CAD served two purposes: validating that all components fit within the intended dimensions, and checking that the lid panel, base panel, jar holders, and glass palette could all coexist in the interior without interference. The model included the glass jars and palette as bodies so their clearances could be verified directly.

Two versions were produced — one showing the box with opaque lids on the jars (as they would normally sit) and one with transparent glass jar bodies visible, to check the recess depths. The lid's circular cutouts in the model correspond to the magnet positions, which were matched to recesses routed in both the lid and the body.

Fabrication

The body walls were cut from ebonised hardwood stock — dark, dense, and finished with a natural oil that deepened the colour. The base panel and lid were cut from a single piece of walnut, chosen for its grain pattern and warm contrast against the dark walls. All pieces were dimensioned on the table saw, then the rabbets were cut using a router table to the tolerances specified in the sketches.

The circular recesses for the glass jars were routed using a forstner bit, sized to hold the jars snugly without glue. Neodymium magnets were press-fit into matching recesses drilled in both the lid and the body — a 7/8" blade was used for the fat magnets, a smaller bit for the skinny rod magnets in the lid. The glass palette sits in a shallow routed channel, retained by the frame walls.

The walnut lid was then laser engraved with Boîte à peinture de Sophia S. Benzari in a serif font, followed by a routed border detail and a final hand-sand before assembly. The brass piano hinge was set into a shallow dado cut along the back edge of both pieces so it sits flush.

Interior

The interior holds three small glass jars in routed circular seats on the right side of the base, with a removable acrylic-protected glass palette occupying the left. The lid interior has matching recesses for the three neodymium magnets that pair with the body magnets to close the box firmly. When the lid is lifted, the magnet pull is satisfying but not excessive — calibrated by the magnet size and the wood thickness above each recess.

Reflections

The joinery phase took longer than any other part of the project. Getting the rabbet depths right so that the walls sat flush, the base panel floated correctly, and the lid closed evenly required several rounds of test cuts on scrap before committing to the final pieces. The floating panel decision in particular — leaving the walnut base panel unglued so it can move with humidity — was a constraint learned from the sketching and research phase, not from prior experience.

The magnet calibration was also non-trivial. The first attempt used magnets that were too strong — the lid would slam shut and was difficult to open single-handed. The final configuration uses a combination of fat and rod magnets at staggered depths to achieve a closure force that holds securely but opens cleanly.

This was a personal project, built because I needed the object. That changes how you approach decisions — there's no rubric, no checkoff, and no one to approve it. You either like it or you don't. I like it.
